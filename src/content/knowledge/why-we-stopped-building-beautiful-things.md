Walk through almost any American city and you can read its history in the buildings. The ones from roughly 1900 to 1930 are carved, molded, bracketed, and corniced. The public library has lions out front. The bank has columns. The train station ceiling is painted with constellations. Even modest houses have porch brackets and patterned shingles. Then, somewhere in the middle of the century, the ornament stops. Buildings become boxes of glass, panels, and stucco, assembled from parts chosen from a catalog.

People notice. In a 2020 Harris Poll commissioned by the National Civic Art Society, 72 percent of Americans said they preferred traditional, classical architecture for federal buildings, a majority that held across race, party, and region.[^harris] So why don't we build it anymore?

Part of the answer is taste, ideology, and history, and we'll get to those. But a large part of it is an economic force that has nothing to do with style at all.

## Baumol's cost disease

In 1966, the economists William Baumol and William Bowen studied why orchestras and theaters always seemed to be in financial trouble. Their answer became one of the most useful ideas in economics. A string quartet in 1966 needed exactly as many musicians, and exactly as many minutes, to play a Schubert quartet as it did in Schubert's day. There's no way to make a live performance more "productive."[^bowen] Meanwhile, factories kept getting faster, and wages everywhere rose with them. Musicians have to be paid enough to keep them from becoming factory workers, so the cost of a concert keeps climbing relative to everything else, even though nothing about the concert has changed. Baumol later generalized the idea to any work whose productivity can't easily rise.[^baumol1967]

Hand carving is a string quartet. A capital carved in 1910 and one carved today take roughly the same hours of skilled work. But in 1910, those hours were cheap relative to everything else. Today they're among the most expensive things in construction.

### Construction caught the disease badly

It isn't just ornament. The construction industry as a whole has barely improved how much it builds per worker in half a century. Economists Austan Goolsbee and Chad Syverson found that construction labor productivity fell by about 1 percent a year from 1970 to 2020. By 2020, it had dropped below its 1950 level. Over the same period, labor productivity across the whole economy rose 290 percent, and manufacturing's rose roughly ninefold.[^goolsbee][^booth]

When the rest of the economy gets that much more productive and your own work doesn't, every hour of it gets relatively more expensive. The obvious response is to use fewer hours: fewer trades on site, more factory-made components, and flat surfaces instead of shaped ones. Ornament is labor made visible, so it went first.

## Baumol wasn't the only cause

Cost disease explains the pressure. It doesn't explain everything about the timing. Several other forces pushed in the same direction:

- **An ideology against ornament.** In 1910, the Viennese architect Adolf Loos delivered his lecture "Ornament and Crime," arguing that decoration was wasteful and backward.[^loos] The idea took root in the architecture schools. In 1932, the Museum of Modern Art's *Modern Architecture: International Exhibition* introduced Americans to the "International Style," and the flat, unornamented box went from fringe to fashionable.[^moma] Within a generation, traditional ornament was treated as kitsch by the profession, even as the public kept liking it.
- **The carvers stopped coming.** Much of America's finest stonework was cut by immigrant craftsmen trained in Europe's carving traditions. The six Piccirilli brothers, sons of a master carver from near the Carrara quarries, carved the Lincoln Memorial's statue, the New York Public Library's lions, and hundreds of other works from their Bronx studio.[^piccirilli] The Immigration Act of 1924 cut Italy's annual quota to 3,845 people. Italian immigration had been averaging about 200,000 a year in the first decade of the century.[^immigration] The pipeline of trained hands narrowed sharply.
- **Depression and war.** The 1929 crash stopped new construction almost overnight. Terra cotta makers like Gladding, McBean closed plants and switched to making dinnerware.[^gladding] Wartime rationing and the postwar rush to house millions of families rewarded speed and standardization above all else.
- **Losing the old, too.** In 1963, New York began demolishing McKim, Mead & White's Pennsylvania Station. The outcry helped create the city's Landmarks Law in 1965 and the National Historic Preservation Act in 1966.[^penn] We learned to protect the buildings of that era, not to build them.

## The golden age was never purely handmade

Here's the part that gets lost in nostalgia: the ornament of 1900 to 1930 wasn't all chiseled by hand. It was the product of the most advanced manufacturing of its day. Architectural terra cotta was cast from molds and could repeat a detailed ornament across an entire skyscraper facade. Millwork was cut by steam-powered machinery and sold from catalogs. Sears sold more than 70,000 precut kit houses between 1908 and 1940, in English cottage, Colonial Revival, and bungalow styles, each shipped by boxcar with every piece numbered.[^sears]

In other words, the golden age combined cheap skilled labor with industrial processes that made beauty repeatable. When the labor got expensive, the industry didn't develop new processes for making ornament. It stopped making ornament.

## How we get it back

That's the hopeful part. If the problem is that ornament depends on labor whose productivity can't rise, the solution is to raise its productivity. The tools to do that finally exist.

- **Robotic stone carving.** Monumental Labs, founded in 2022 by the tech entrepreneur and hobbyist carver Micah Springut, uses industrial robot arms with diamond-tipped tools to rough out sculpture and architectural stone, which human carvers then finish by hand.[^humanprogress] It has marketed custom marble sculptures at prices once unthinkable for hand carving.[^bloomberg] It has also produced stonework for restorations of landmarks like Carnegie Hall and the Frick Collection, as well as new traditional buildings.[^archrecord] The machine does the hours of removing stone. The artisan does the part that takes judgment.
- **CNC woodwork.** Computer-controlled routers can cut brackets, corbels, balusters, tracery, and carved panels from a digital file in minutes, and then cut the next hundred identically. A small shop with a CNC machine can produce the kind of millwork that once required a factory of skilled workers, and it can customize every piece.
- **Digital molds and casting.** Ornament can be designed digitally, 3D-printed or milled into molds, and cast in glass-fiber reinforced concrete, cast stone, or new terra cotta. That's the golden-age method, with a computer replacing the sculptor who shaped the original model.
- **Scanning and pattern libraries.** Historic ornament can be laser-scanned from existing buildings and shared as open digital libraries, so a builder in any city can reproduce a cornice, capital, or bracket without starting from scratch.
- **Rebuilding the craft.** Machines rough out the work, but people still design, finish, fit, and repair it. Apprenticeships and trade programs that pair digital tools with traditional skills create carvers and joiners who can work many times faster than their predecessors.

None of this requires anyone to give up modern construction. It means treating ornament as a manufacturing problem again: something to be engineered, tooled, and made affordable, rather than a luxury to be cut from the budget.

## What we lost, and what we can choose

Cost disease didn't make Americans stop wanting beautiful buildings. The polls say we still do. It made beauty expensive relative to everything else, at the exact moment the profession decided beauty was beside the point, and the supply of craftsmen was cut off. The result is a century of cities built to the lowest cost per square foot, in which the only buildings people travel to admire were built before most of them were born.

The economics that drove ornament out are finally starting to turn. Robots can remove stone, routers can carve wood, and a mold can be made from a file. Whether we use them to build places as good as the ones we inherited is no longer a question of whether we can afford it. It's a question of whether we want to.
