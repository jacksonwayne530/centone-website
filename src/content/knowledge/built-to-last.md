The buildings people love most are, almost by definition, the ones that survived. The brick warehouse turned into lofts, the main street storefront on its fifth business, the church that became a library. Nobody planned for any of that. The buildings simply lasted long enough, and were sturdy and simple enough, for each new generation to find a use for them.

Most of what we build today isn't made to get that chance. It's made to be sold, refinanced, or depreciated within a decade or two. That's not because we've forgotten how to build durably. It's because almost every incentive in modern development points toward the short term.

## Buildings rarely fall down. We tear them down.

In the early 2000s, researchers from Forintek Canada and the Athena Institute surveyed 227 buildings demolished in Minneapolis and St. Paul between 2000 and 2003. They wanted to know how long buildings actually last, and why they come down. The answer had very little to do with what they were made of. Only eight of the 227 were demolished because of a specific problem with their structure or materials. Most of the steel and concrete buildings in the study were less than 50 years old when they were torn down.[^athena]

Buildings die of three things: the land under them becomes worth more than they are, they can't adapt to what people need now, or nobody maintains them. Durable materials don't protect against any of those on their own. A building built for the long haul has to be durable, adaptable, and worth taking care of, all at once.

## How buildings learn

In 1994, Stewart Brand, the founder of the *Whole Earth Catalog*, published *How Buildings Learn: What Happens After They're Built*. Instead of photographing buildings on the day they opened, he went back to see what had happened to them over decades: who added on, who tore out walls, which buildings adapted gracefully and which ones fought their owners every step of the way.[^brand] Its central warning fits on a business card: "All buildings are predictions. All predictions are wrong."

Brand's most useful idea, borrowed and expanded from the British architect Frank Duffy, is that a building isn't one thing. It's a stack of layers that change at different speeds:[^layers]

The trouble starts when the layers are locked together. When the plumbing is buried in a structural wall, replacing a pipe means demolition. When the skin is also the structure, you can't update one without the other. When the floor plan is set by load-bearing walls, the building can only ever be what it was first designed to be. The fast layers wear out or go out of date, and because they can't be changed on their own, the whole building goes with them.

Brand saw two kinds of buildings that age well. "High Road" buildings are beautiful enough, and cared for well enough, that generations of owners are willing to pour money into keeping them. "Low Road" buildings are plain, cheap, and roomy, like warehouses and garages, so nobody minds when the next tenant knocks out a wall. The buildings that age badly are the ones he called "magazine architecture": designed for the photograph on opening day, inflexible, and expensive to fix.[^brand]

The lesson for anyone building something new: **make the slow layers last for centuries, and make the fast layers easy to change.** The British architect Alex Gordon put the same idea more briefly in 1972, when he defined good architecture as "long life, loose fit, low energy."[^gordon] Brand later broadened the idea into "pace layering" for whole societies. The fast layers innovate. The slow layers hold everything steady.[^pace] Cities work the same way. Businesses turn over, buildings last longer, the street grid lasts longest of all.

## Why we build for thirty years instead of three hundred

If durable, adaptable buildings are so obviously better, why don't we build them? Because the people who decide what gets built are rarely the people who will own it in year fifty, and the systems they work within don't reward year fifty at all.

### The money doesn't see that far

**Developers build to sell.** Much of new construction is built by merchant developers who plan to sell within a few years of finishing, once the building is leased up. The equity behind them often comes from closed-end funds with a life of seven to ten years.[^funds] Every dollar spent on a longer-lasting roof, a better wall, or a bigger floor-to-floor height has to be earned back by the time they sell, and the next buyer will only pay for what shows up in the next ten years of rent.

**Discounting erases the future.** Investors value a building by discounting its future income back to today. At an 8 percent discount rate, which is in the range commonly used for new development, a dollar of rent in year 50 is worth about two cents today. A dollar in year 100 is worth less than a tenth of a cent. A building that lasts an extra century is, on paper, worth almost nothing more.

**Debt runs on short clocks.** Construction loans last a few years. Permanent loans that replace them commonly mature in 5 to 10 years. The most common Fannie Mae multifamily loan is a 10-year balloon.[^dus] Every maturity is a moment when the building has to be refinanced or sold, and a moment when the owner is tempted to cut maintenance to make the numbers work.

**The tax code assumes buildings wear out.** The IRS depreciates residential rental buildings over 27.5 years and commercial buildings over 39.[^irs] Those schedules are a tax convenience, not an engineering estimate, but they shape how owners think about a building: as an asset that's used up within a generation and then sold to someone who can depreciate it all over again.

### The culture treats buildings as products

Somewhere in the last century we started treating buildings like cars: a product with a model year, built to a price, expected to be replaced. Construction contracts usually go to the lowest bidder. "Value engineering" mostly means swapping durable materials for cheaper ones that look the same on opening day: stone and brick become thin veneers, solid wood becomes composites, real stucco becomes foam. Each swap saves money now and spends more of it later, usually on someone else's watch.

Maintenance has the same problem. It's invisible when it's done well and expensive when it's skipped, and it almost never makes it into a ribbon-cutting photo. In the Twin Cities study, lack of maintenance was by far the most common explanation behind "physical condition" demolitions.[^athena] We lost the habit of building something and then simply caring for it, the way our grandparents expected to.

### Momentum keeps us building what we built last year

The last obstacle is momentum. Once a building type is financeable, everyone builds it. Lenders know how to underwrite it, appraisers have comparables for it, insurers have priced it, contractors have crews trained for it, and code officials have approved it a hundred times. That's how the "five-over-one," five stories of light wood framing on a concrete podium, came to define new apartment buildings across the country. It's a reasonable type, but most of these buildings are built to the same short horizon. Something different, even something better, means convincing every one of those parties to do something they haven't done before. The building you can get financed is usually the one that got financed last year.

## How we build for the long haul

None of these obstacles is a law of nature. Each one points to something we can change.

- **Separate the layers.** Build the structure for centuries, then make everything faster than it easy to reach and replace. Keep pipes and wires in accessible shafts and ceilings, not buried in walls. Hang the skin on the structure so it can be repaired panel by panel. Use a regular column grid instead of interior bearing walls, so the floor plan can change.
- **Build loose.** Generous floor-to-floor heights and open floor plates let a building be apartments now, offices later, and something we haven't thought of after that. A tall ground floor can be a shop, a restaurant, a clinic, or a lobby. Adaptability is what lets a building survive its first use.
- **Put the toughest materials where the wear is.** The first two floors take the abuse: hands, bikes, carts, road salt, splashing water, and graffiti. That's where stone and masonry earn their cost. Higher up, the priorities are keeping water out and the structure dry.
- **Keep it simple and drained.** Simple shapes, generous overhangs, details that shed water and let walls dry out. Complicated shapes are where leaks start, and leaks are where buildings start to die.
- **Find patient capital.** Family offices, endowments, pension funds, cooperatives, and community land trusts can hold a building for decades. Long-term debt helps too: HUD's Section 221(d)(4) program insures 40-year, fixed-rate, fully amortizing loans for new apartment buildings, after up to three years of interest-only construction.[^hud] A 40-year loan doesn't force a sale or a refinance in year ten.
- **Fund maintenance from day one.** Capital reserves sized for a building's whole life, not just its first loan, turn maintenance from a crisis into a routine.
- **Make the rules reward longevity.** Whole-life carbon accounting, life-cycle cost analysis in public projects, and codes that make adaptable structures easy to approve all push the market toward buildings that last.

## Case study: a ten-story building for the next two centuries

Here's what those principles look like in one building: a ten-story mixed-use building on a main-street corner. It's a two-story masonry podium clad in basalt, topped by an eight-story tower of cross-laminated timber (CLT), with a landscaped terrace on the roof.

**The podium: masonry and stone at street level.** The first two floors are reinforced concrete block bearing walls with concrete columns, clad in basalt. Basalt is a dense volcanic stone that shrugs off the scuffs, salt, and weather that destroy softer finishes at sidewalk level, and it only gets better looking as it wears. The ground floor is 17 feet tall, enough for almost any commercial use, and the second floor works as offices or apartments. The stone is hung on anchors rather than glued on, so it's a true skin: a cracked panel comes out and a new one goes in.

**The tower: timber, kept dry.** Above the podium, eight stories of CLT floor panels sit on glue-laminated timber columns around a central core. Mass timber this tall is a proven building type. The 2021 International Building Code added construction types that allow mass timber buildings up to 18 stories,[^ibc] and the code's podium provisions let a timber building stand on a concrete base separated by a 3-hour-rated slab.[^podium] Brock Commons, an 18-story student residence at the University of British Columbia, was built from CLT floors on glulam columns in 2017.[^brock] Ascent, a 25-story apartment tower in Milwaukee, opened in 2022 on a concrete podium.[^ascent]

Wood that stays dry lasts a very long time. Several buildings at the Horyu-ji temple in Japan date from the late 7th or 8th century and are among the oldest wooden buildings in the world.[^horyuji] The design's job is to keep water away from the timber for good: a ventilated terracotta rainscreen sheds rain before it reaches the wall, a drained cavity lets anything that gets through escape, and the roof is covered and protected by the terrace above.

**Loose fit inside.** The tower has no interior bearing walls. Apartments are laid out with ordinary partitions that can be moved, so units can be combined for families or split for students, or whole floors converted to offices if the neighborhood changes. Every pipe, duct, and cable runs up through the core, where it can be reached through access doors on each floor and replaced when it wears out, every 15 to 30 years, without touching the structure.

**A terrace on top.** The roof is a shared garden with planters, trees, a lawn, and a pergola. It's an amenity residents will care about, which matters more than it sounds: people maintain what they love. The soil and planting also shield the roof membrane from sun and temperature swings and slow down stormwater.

**What it costs, and how to pay for it.** This building costs more up front than a five-over-one. Basalt and terracotta cost more than fiber cement panels or stucco, CLT and glulam usually cost more than light wood framing, and generous floor heights add wall area. Some of that comes back during construction: CLT panels arrive cut to size and go up quickly, and Brock Commons' entire timber superstructure went up in about 70 days.[^brock] The rest comes back slowly, in lower maintenance, fewer replacements, and a building that can still earn rent when its neighbors have been torn down.

That only pencils for an owner who plans to be around to collect. Paired with a 40-year HUD loan, a long-term owner, and a reserve fund sized for the building's whole life, the extra up-front cost becomes an investment that pays out over decades. It's the same math that built the main streets we still love, before the incentives pointed somewhere else.

## Building like we mean it

A building that lasts isn't just a better asset. It's a better neighbor. It gives a street continuity, a place for new businesses to start in old storefronts, and a patina that new construction can't fake. It also saves the carbon and the waste of tearing down and starting over every few decades.

Our cities are quilts pieced together over generations. Every building we put up becomes one of the pieces our grandchildren inherit. We can keep making pieces designed to be thrown away in thirty years, or we can make pieces worth mending.
