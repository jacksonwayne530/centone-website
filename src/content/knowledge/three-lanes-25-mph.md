Here's a claim that sounds radical and turns out to be pretty conservative: almost no street in a city needs more than three lanes, two travel lanes plus a center turn lane, or a speed limit above 25 miles per hour.

By "street" I mean the surface roads where people live, shop, cross, and wait for the bus. Urban freeways are a separate argument. But the typical American arterial, four to six lanes with a posted limit of 35 to 45 mph and lined with driveways, bus stops, and crosswalks, is the most dangerous kind of road we build. It asks the same space to be both a highway and a main street, and it does both badly.

The toll is visible. U.S. drivers killed 7,148 pedestrians in 2024. That was an improvement on the 40-year high set in 2022, but still about 20 percent above 2016. Between 2009 and 2023, pedestrian deaths rose 80 percent, while all other traffic deaths rose 13 percent.[^ghsa]

## Speed decides who survives

The single most important fact about street safety is how sharply the risk of death rises with impact speed. The AAA Foundation's analysis of U.S. crash data found that a pedestrian's average risk of death is 10 percent at 23 mph, 25 percent at 32 mph, 50 percent at 42 mph, 75 percent at 50 mph, and 90 percent at 58 mph.[^tefft] Age makes it worse. A 70-year-old struck at 25 mph faces about the same risk of severe injury or death as a 30-year-old struck at 35.

In other words, 25 mph is roughly where a mistake by a driver, a child, or anyone else is usually survivable. At 40 mph, the same mistake is a coin flip.

### Lower limits work

A common objection is that changing the sign doesn't change how people drive. The evidence says otherwise. When Boston cut its default limit from 30 to 25 mph in 2017, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety compared speeds against a control city. The odds of a vehicle going faster than 35 mph fell by 29.3 percent, with no new street redesign at all.[^iihs]

Cities that pair lower limits with street design are seeing striking results:

- **Helsinki** lowered limits to 30 km/h (about 19 mph) on more than half its streets, alongside redesigned intersections, more cycling and transit, and more speed cameras. In August 2025, it reported 12 months without a single traffic death.[^helsinki]
- **Bologna** became a "Città 30," a 30 km/h city, in January 2024. In the first year, road deaths fell by nearly half, and for the first time since 1991 no pedestrian was killed on its streets.[^bologna]
- **Wales** made 20 mph the default on built-up roads in September 2023. In the first year, about 100 fewer people were killed or seriously injured on its 20 and 30 mph roads.[^wales]

### The rule that keeps speeds high

Most American speed limits are still set by the "85th percentile rule": measure how fast drivers actually go, then set the limit near the speed that 85 percent of them don't exceed. It's circular. Streets designed for speed produce fast drivers, which justifies higher limits. The National Transportation Safety Board found in 2017 that there isn't enough evidence the rule works. Raising limits to match the 85th percentile can raise speeds overall, the Board found, and it recommended including crash history and the presence of people walking and cycling instead.[^ntsb] The National Association of City Transportation Officials now recommends default limits of 25 mph on major city streets and 20 mph on minor ones.[^nacto-limits]

### The time cost is smaller than it looks

Driving two miles at 35 mph takes about 3.4 minutes. At 25 mph it takes about 4.8. That's a difference of less than a minute and a half even in perfect conditions. In real cities, where traffic lights and congestion set the pace, the difference is usually smaller still. In exchange, a crash with someone on foot goes from probably fatal to probably survivable.

## Why three lanes is enough

### A three-lane street carries more than you'd think

The Federal Highway Administration has studied "road diets" for decades. The most common version converts a four-lane undivided street into two travel lanes and a center turn lane. The FHWA finds these conversions reduce total crashes by 19 to 47 percent, and it considers roads carrying up to about 20,000 vehicles per day good candidates.[^fhwa]

The reason is that the inside lanes of a four-lane undivided road already work as turn lanes: every left-turning car stops traffic behind it. Give turning cars their own lane and the street carries nearly as much traffic, with fewer rear-end crashes, fewer sideswipes from lane changes, and one fewer lane for people to cross on foot.

### Wider roads fill up

Adding lanes to relieve congestion doesn't work for long. Economists Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner found that vehicle miles traveled on metropolitan interstates rise roughly one-for-one with new lane-miles. They found suggestive evidence that the same "fundamental law of road congestion" applies to major urban roads generally.[^duranton] New capacity attracts new driving until the road is congested again. A six-lane arterial isn't a solution to traffic. It's a larger version of the same traffic.

### More lanes, more crashes

In their study of 24 California cities, Wesley Marshall and Norman Garrick found that more travel lanes on major streets were associated with more crashes. Denser, better-connected street networks were associated with fewer.[^marshall] That points to the real reason most streets don't need to be wide: in a well-connected grid, traffic spreads across many parallel streets, so no single one has to act as a highway. (More on that in [Count the Intersections](/knowledge/count-the-intersections/).)

### Use the width for people

If a corridor really does need to move more people, a wider general traffic lane is the least efficient way to do it. The National Association of City Transportation Officials estimates that a single urban lane moves 600 to 1,600 people an hour in private cars. A two-way protected bike lane can move about 7,500. A dedicated bus lane can move up to 8,000, and a dedicated transitway up to 25,000.[^nacto-transit] Streets that need more capacity than three lanes provide should get it as bus lanes, bike lanes, and wider sidewalks, not more lanes of cars.

## What about...

**Trucks and deliveries?** Trucks serve two-lane main streets all over the world. A center turn lane and designated loading zones handle deliveries better than a wide road where double-parking blocks a travel lane.

**Fire trucks and ambulances?** A center turn lane gives emergency vehicles a clear path through traffic. A connected street grid gives them multiple routes to every address.

**The exceptions?** They exist, which is why the claim says *almost*. A few major corridors may be wider than three lanes, but the extra width should go to transit, bikes, trees, and sidewalks, not general traffic.

## What cities can do

1. **Set 25 mph as the citywide default,** and 20 mph on residential streets and around schools, following NACTO's guidance.
2. **Design streets to match the limit.** Narrow the lanes, add street trees, and use raised crosswalks and curb extensions, so that 25 feels natural rather than enforced.
3. **Do road diets at repaving time.** Restriping a four-lane street into three when it's resurfaced anyway costs very little.
4. **Use automated speed enforcement** on the corridors where speeding and crashes concentrate.
5. **Build connected networks,** so traffic has many routes and no street has to carry all of it.

A three-lane street with a 25 mph limit isn't a compromise between safety and mobility. For nearly every street in a city, it's the design that delivers both.
